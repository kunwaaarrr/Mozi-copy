# 07 — Reframing (3A) & Negotiation

Two related skills: **reframing** keeps you in the conversation after anything-but-yes; **negotiation** structures the deal itself. Both are about controlling the frame instead of accepting the one you're handed.

---

## Why reframing matters more than the comeback

A **reframe is the 1–3 sentences you say after a prospect says anything but yes** that make whatever you say *next* more likely to land. The most productive closers ask for the sale more times than anyone else — but asking the *wrong* way costs rapport, and without rapport you can't ask again. The reframe is what lets you ask unlimited times. Beginners fire overcomes straight back ("seems combative"); pros reframe first. *(yt/04)*

**The person asking the questions is in control.** ("I'm the one asking the questions here.") The moment you start answering, you're on defense and they're the judge of your answers — and with a trap question you don't even know what the right answer is. So: never answer a trap question — ask a question about the question, then chase the reason, then answer the *reason*:

> *"How many certifications do your trainers have?"* → *"Which certifications are you looking for specifically?"* → "NASM, ACE." → *"Why those specifically?"* → they give the reason → now either *"yeah, we chose these for exactly that reason"* or *"we use different ones, for this reason — feel better?"* You asked your way out of ever answering blind; the control flipped on the first question. Same with *"How many support emails do I get?"* → *"How many questions do you want to ask — what are you actually trying to make sure of?"* This works at $100 and at $1M+, in person, by phone, from stage. *(yt/04)*

**No one disagrees with a question.** You can never be wrong if you're asking. *"I don't have time"* → *"Totally understand. Is it a seasonal thing? …What would make it a good time?"* You're not arguing; there's nothing to push against. Be like smoke — they can't catch you (law 3 in `01-core-principles.md`). *(yt/04, yt/03)*

## The 3A framework

**1. Acknowledge.** Say the last thing they said back to them. Buys you 2–3 seconds to think; signals active listening (which you should actually be doing); keeps rapport intact. The all-purpose version is just *"huh"* — head tilted, genuinely curious: *"Huh — that's so interesting you'd ask that. Can I ask you a little more about that?"* Even for nasty questions.
> "Timing is kind of tough for you right now."

**2. Associate.** Attach what they said to the *behavior of your best customers* — so the step they think is *away* from buying becomes evidence they're exactly the type who succeeds:
> "That's an amazing question — honestly it's the question our best customers ask." / "I think it's smart you're already thinking about implementation — our biggest success stories thought it through exactly like you're doing."

Three association types:
- **Generic positive** — "smart question," "totally understandable," "super fair."
- **Customer foil** — "someone I talked to this morning had the same concern — can I share what I told them?" Lets you deliver a harsh truth *indirectly*: you're not attacking them, you're quoting what you told someone like them. Foils protect egos so you can be more direct than you ever could head-on.
- **Authority foil** — "our senior engineer told me this just today…" / "our founder says…" Borrowed authority for when you don't outrank the prospect.

**3. Ask.** Move forward with the next question. Never end a reframe with a statement.
> "So what would make it a good time?" / "Out of curiosity — which parts do you think your spouse wouldn't like?"

**Worked examples** *(yt/04)*:
- "I don't have time / timing's off" → "Totally understand — timing's tough right now. Honestly, I think it's smart you're thinking about implementation; our biggest successes did exactly that. **So what would make it a good time?**"
- "I need to talk to my spouse" → "Really sensible — super fair. Just so I understand, out of curiosity: **which parts do you think they wouldn't like?**" (You're back in the sale, attacking named concerns instead of a ghost — full decision-maker sequence in `06-objection-closes.md` §6.)
- "I hate this particular feature" → "Totally get it — I hate them all too. *Kidding.* Real question: **why not?**"
- The reframe used *on Hormozi* (private wealth pitch): "I already have a wealth advisor, I'm good." → *"That's amazing — 90% of the clients who move over to us already have one."* "I thought I was getting out of this, and now I'm *more like the customers who buy.*"

**Labeling (the Associate zoom-in):** hand out labels early, harvest them at the close. They ask a sharp question → "that's a really smart question — shows you're a rational person making a serious decision" → at the close, *smart people do this*. They raise a spouse concern → "sounds like family's really important to you — that's admirable" → at the close, *this is what a good family man does for his family*. Tell them what their question *means* about them — generously — and you get to pair every obstacle with its own close. Requires real rapport or it reads as manipulation. *(yt/04)*

**The two power questions, by depth:** early or first objection → *"What's your main concern?"* Deeper in, when they're circling → *"What are you afraid of having happen?"* — it cuts to the heart faster, but you have to have earned it; asked cold it reads as pressure. Then: *"Let's just play it out."* (full play-it-out close in `06-objection-closes.md`). Rapport first, hard questions second. *(yt/04)*

**The rules that keep it ethical** *(yt/04)*:
1. **State the facts and tell the truth.** Reframing organizes truth; it never replaces it.
2. **Only sell qualified prospects** — if they can't genuinely benefit, stop.
3. **Seek to understand, not to win.** You're never caught off guard — you've had this conversation a thousand times; they've had it once. If resistance rattles you, you've misunderstood the job.
4. **Keep the human number one.** (Sticky note above the camera.) Care about the prospect more than the close and you will never come across pushy — they can hear intent in your tone. Keep a mirror by the phone: whether you're smiling or not, your voice comes out different.
5. **You don't close by being right.** Win the argument, lose the sale. The job is to make *them* right about a now-logical decision to buy.

**Before getting real, get permission.** When a hard truth is needed: *"Can I be a coach instead of a friend for a second?" / "Do you mind if I get real with you?" / "Would it be totally overstepping if I shared something that might be uncomfortable?"* If you don't have the standing (you're 22 selling to a $20M founder), use the foils instead — "can I tell you what I told someone in your exact position?" If a close ever feels unsayable, it's because the 3A + permission step was skipped. *(yt/01, yt/04)*

**The stance that makes all of it land — the time-travel frame:** imagine you've gone back in time in a different body, you can see your old self, and you *know the future* — and you have to convince them to buy Apple stock or Bitcoin. They don't know who you are. Would you argue? You'd be patient, empathetic, certain: *"I totally understand this seems crazy — let me walk you through what I think will happen."* That's every sale: you know how the program turns out; they can't yet. And the verbal posture that goes with it: *"I'm just the guide here — I'm just going to share information that I think will help you make a good decision."* You're not the hero proving you're right; they're the decision-maker. Lowers the stakes for everyone. *(yt/04)*

## Negotiation: the street-tested tactics

Contexts: employees ↔ employers (both directions), vendors ↔ customers, partners/deals/M&A. "You get what you negotiate, not what you deserve." *(yt/06)*

1. **BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement.** The single greatest source of psychological power, and it's won **before you sit down at the table**. Negotiators who know their alternatives set higher aspirations, make more aggressive first offers, and get better outcomes; your BATNA is the decision standard — only accept deals better than your best alternative. *"You only want to sell when you don't want to sell; you want to buy when you don't want to buy."* In practice: employee → get another offer *first*, then negotiate (sparingly — it spends goodwill); buying from vendors → collect 4–6 bids (which also *educates you on terms other people include*, feeding tactic 4); selling/vendor side → your BATNA is your other customers: *"if you don't want it, no worries — I've got another customer behind you."* Use competing offers overtly ("beat it or no worries") or just hold them in mind and swing higher: negotiating without an alternative is playing poker where your only move is bluffing. *(yt/06)*
2. **Anchor first, anchor high, and anchor the increments.** The anchor is the first number said — by anyone. (Saying "I'm not trying to anchor here" doesn't un-anchor it.) Counter-anchoring is the weaker move you're left with if they go first. Anchoring works because people give excessive weight to initial information and adjust insufficiently from it (Kahneman) — so **get the gasp**: the big number shifts the *increments* everyone thinks in. Quote $100K to someone expecting $10K and their counter arrives in $20K steps. Your concession sizes anchor too — they signal how much room is left. Live example: house listed $25M → dropped to $20M → Hormozi offers $15M → they counter $17M (moved $3M) → he counters **$15.25M** (moved $250K = "I'm nearly done moving") and sweetens with *terms instead of price*: all cash, **and** the $4M of furniture included — technically a tougher offer wearing a friendlier face. Move little; stack terms. *(yt/06)*
3. **Ascribe a price to their mess-up — then flip it.** Something's wrong with the goods? Don't ask for a discount. Ask: *"How much would it cost **you** to fix this?"* They inflate it ("oh, we'd have to go back to the shop… probably $1,500"). → *"That sounds like a pretty good place to start the discount."* They bid against themselves; backing down from their own number is nearly impossible. *(yt/06)*
4. **MESOs — Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers.** Present offers A, B, C with different price/term mixes, all acceptable to you: *"All three work for me — I just want to know which works best for you."* It embeds reciprocity (you look reasonable), it's a *reverse assumed close* (every option they can pick is a yes), and their pick **reveals their true priorities** without them having to show their cards. On the receiving end: *"I like the best part of this one, and this one, and this one — why don't we make an offer that's the best of all three?"* Or invite it: *"Can you give me two or three versions of what this deal might look like?"* — then counter with option D built from the pieces that were cheap to them. *(yt/06)*
5. **Reciprocity + horse-trading with the full variable set.** Two caveats first: reciprocity only operates where it's the cultural norm, and it trades on *perceived* value, not actual — carrying someone's lunch tray doesn't buy an airport pickup; mismatched-size "trades" smell manipulative. The pro move: **break the deal into as many pieces as possible so you can trade more times.** Amateurs see two variables (price, yes/no); keep a deal sheet with ~80 (speed, risk allocation, ease, cash vs financed, closing window, inclusions, support, exclusivity…). Then concede in *your* cheap currency while holding price: "I'll do 15 with ease" → they drop 17→16 → "15 with ease and risk" → 16→15.5 → "15 with ease, risk, and speed." Stay in reciprocity, never touch the number. Trade things cheap to you and dear to them for the reverse — that's what makes negotiation **positive-sum**: interlock the asymmetries and both sides genuinely win. *(yt/06)*
6. **Framing: investment vs cost.** Whoever sets the frame usually keeps it — most people just accept the frame they're handed. Selling yourself or your service: *"I'm an investment, not a cost — here's the return."* ("For a $5,000 investment you get $15,000 in maintenance savings" ≠ "this costs $5,000," even though they're identical.) Buying: reframe their thing as cost/overhead to push it down. **Bring data for your frame**: the pool that costs $100K and adds $100K of home value *is free — and you got to swim in it the whole time.* "The houses selling at $1.2M all have pools; yours is worth $1M." Even at half the ratio, you're framing "half off plus you enjoy it the whole time," not "$100K." Frame in returns, prove with comps. *(yt/06)*

**After the handshake, make them feel classy.** When you accept an offer fast (because they anchored low), put them at ease: *"If you were wondering whether I'd have done it for less — I wouldn't have."* The penthouse seller who told Hormozi *"we wouldn't have sold it for a penny less"* — maybe true, maybe not, but it felt classy and cost nothing. A negotiation win shouldn't leave the other side replaying the tape. *(yt/06)*
