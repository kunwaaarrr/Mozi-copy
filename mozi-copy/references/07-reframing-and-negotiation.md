# 07 — Reframing (3A) & Negotiation

Two related skills: **reframing** keeps you in the conversation after anything-but-yes; **negotiation** structures the deal itself. Both are about controlling the frame instead of accepting the one you're handed.

---

## Why reframing matters more than the comeback

A **reframe is the 1–3 sentences you say after a prospect says anything but yes** that make whatever you say *next* more likely to land. The most productive closers ask for the sale more times than anyone else — but asking the *wrong* way costs rapport, and without rapport you can't ask again. The reframe is what lets you ask unlimited times. Beginners fire overcomes straight back ("seems combative"); pros reframe first. *(yt/04)*

**The person asking the questions is in control.** ("I'm the one asking the questions here.") The moment you start answering, you're on defense and they're the judge of your answers. So: never answer a trap question — ask a question about the question. *"How many certifications do your trainers have?"* → *"Which certifications are you looking for specifically?"* *"How many support emails do I get?"* → *"How many questions do you want to ask — what are you actually trying to make sure of?"* This works at $100 and at $1M+, in person, by phone, from stage. *(yt/04)*

## The 3A framework

**1. Acknowledge.** Say the last thing they said back to them. Buys you a beat to think; signals active listening (which you should actually be doing); keeps rapport intact.
> "Timing is kind of tough for you right now."

**2. Associate.** Attach what they said to the *behavior of your best customers* — so the step they think is *away* from buying becomes evidence they're exactly the type who succeeds:
> "That's an amazing question — honestly it's the question our best customers ask." / "I think it's smart you're already thinking about implementation — our biggest success stories thought it through exactly like you're doing."

Three association types:
- **Generic positive** — "smart question," "totally understandable," "super fair."
- **Customer foil** — "someone I talked to this morning had the same concern — can I share what I told them?" Lets you deliver a harsh truth *indirectly*: you're not attacking them, you're quoting what you told someone like them. Foils protect egos so you can be more direct than you ever could head-on.
- **Authority foil** — "our senior engineer told me this just today…" / "our founder says…" Borrowed authority for when you don't outrank the prospect.

**3. Ask.** Move forward with the next question. Never end a reframe with a statement.
> "So what would make it a good time?" / "Out of curiosity — which parts do you think your spouse wouldn't like?"

**Worked examples** *(yt/04)*:
- "I don't have time / timing's off" → "Totally understand — timing's tough right now. Honestly, I think it's smart you're thinking about implementation; our biggest successes did exactly that. **So what would make it a good time?**"
- "I need to talk to my spouse" → "Really sensible — super fair. Just so I understand, out of curiosity: **which parts do you think they wouldn't like?**" (You're back in the sale, attacking named concerns instead of a ghost — full decision-maker sequence in `06-objection-closes.md` §6.)
- "I hate this particular feature" → "Totally get it — I hate them all too. *Kidding.* Real question: **why not?**"
- The reframe used *on Hormozi* (private wealth pitch): "I already have a wealth advisor, I'm good." → *"That's amazing — 90% of the clients who move over to us already have one."* "I thought I was getting out of this, and now I'm *more like the customers who buy.*"

**Labeling (the Associate zoom-in):** hand out labels early, harvest them at the close. They ask a sharp question → "that's a really smart question" → at the close, *smart people do this*. They raise a spouse concern → "sounds like family's really important to you — that's admirable" → at the close, *this is what a good family man does for his family*. Tell them what their question *means* about them — generously — and you get to pair every obstacle with its own close. Requires real rapport or it reads as manipulation. *(yt/04)*

**The rules that keep it ethical** *(yt/04)*:
1. **State the facts and tell the truth.** Reframing organizes truth; it never replaces it.
2. **Only sell qualified prospects** — if they can't genuinely benefit, stop.
3. **Seek to understand, not to win.** You're never caught off guard — you've had this conversation a thousand times; they've had it once. If resistance rattles you, you've misunderstood the job.
4. **Keep the human number one.** (Sticky note above the camera.) Care about the prospect more than the close and you will never come across pushy — they can hear intent in your tone.
5. **You don't close by being right.** Win the argument, lose the sale.

**Before getting real, get permission.** When a hard truth is needed: *"Can I be a coach instead of a friend for a second?" / "Do you mind if I get real with you?" / "Would it be totally overstepping if I shared something that might be uncomfortable?"* If you don't have the standing (you're 22 selling to a $20M founder), use the foils instead — "can I tell you what I told someone in your exact position?" If a close ever feels unsayable, it's because the 3A + permission step was skipped. *(yt/01)*

## Negotiation: the five street-tested tactics

Contexts: employees ↔ employers, vendors ↔ customers, partners/deals. *(yt/06 — note: tactic 1's full segment is partially lost to a transcription error in the source; what survives is below.)*

1. **Anchor with alternatives, anchor asymmetrically.** Walk in holding one or more genuinely compelling alternative offers — that's the psychological power that lets you **anchor high on your initial ask and low on your counters**, conceding in small steps. When you do move, *sweeten with terms instead of price*: the $15.25M counter became "all cash — and I want the $4M of furniture included," technically a tougher offer wearing a friendlier face. Move little; stack terms.
2. **Ascribe a price to their mess-up — then flip it.** Something's wrong with the goods? Don't ask for a discount. Ask: *"How much would it cost **you** to fix this?"* They inflate it ("oh, we'd have to go back to the shop… probably $1,500"). → *"That sounds like a pretty good place to start the discount."* They bid against themselves; backing down from their own number is nearly impossible.
3. **MESOs — Multiple Equivalent Simultaneous Offers.** Present offers A, B, C with different price/term mixes, all acceptable to you: *"All three work for me — I just want to know which works best for you."* It embeds reciprocity (you look reasonable), and their pick **reveals their true priorities** without them having to tell you. Counter-move if you're on the receiving end: *"I like the best part of this one, and this one, and this one"* — cherry-pick across offers.
4. **Horse-trade with the full variable set.** Amateurs see two variables (price, yes/no); you have eighty (timing, scope, cash vs terms, inclusions, support, exclusivity…). Trade things cheap to you and dear to them for things dear to you and cheap to them — that's what makes negotiation **positive-sum**, not zero-sum: interlock the asymmetries and both sides genuinely win.
5. **Framing: investment vs cost.** Whoever sets the frame usually keeps it — most people just accept the frame they're handed. Selling yourself or your service: *"I'm an investment, not a cost — here's the return."* ("For a $5,000 investment you get $15,000 in maintenance savings" ≠ "this costs $5,000," even though they're identical.) Buying: reframe their thing as cost/overhead to push it down. **Bring data for your frame**: the pool that costs $100K and adds $100K of home value *is free — and you got to swim in it the whole time.* "Look at the ten comparable houses that sold — the ones without it took this loss." Frame in returns, prove with comps.
